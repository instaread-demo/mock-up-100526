Amon Amarth

Written by burning ambulance

The first time I saw Amon Amarth live was in May 2003. They were part of an ill-fated package tour headlined by Testament and then-former Judas Priest singer Rob Halford’s solo band. Amon Amarth were on early, right before black metallers Immortal. Their short set (five songs, probably?) was head-down and focused; frontman Johan Hegg was a burly, hair-flailing presence, leaning out over the crowd to gesture with muscular arms as he barked the words. I was very close to the front and was immediately impressed.

The second time was on my birthday in 2006, at the Nokia Theatre; they were supporting Children Of Bodom, and Gojira and Sanctity opened. They were still climbing the ladder, a tight, aggressive band with plenty to prove, but the crowd was with them all the way. I remember being really into Gojira that night, too. (Years later, I’d work with Gojira at Roadrunner Records.)

The third time was in February 2014, at Irving Plaza; they had long since graduated to headlining status, and frankly could probably have played a bigger room. They had support from Enslaved and Skeletonwitch, both of whom were just OK. By that time, Hegg had evolved from a gruff warrior to a Falstaffian entertainer and party host, drinking from a horn between songs and exhorting the crowd to roar between songs.

The last time I saw Amon Amarth live was in October 2019, at the cavernous Hammerstein Ballroom. They were headlining again, and had put together an all-Swedish package that included Arch Enemy, At The Gates, and Grand Magus. I never thought I’d have the chance to see Grand Magus live — they’re not popular enough in the US to justify a tour — so I got tickets immediately, and they were the band whose T-shirt I bought. Amon Amarth’s stage set was gigantic and elaborate, worthy of an even bigger band, and they had fully embraced Viking kitsch, adopting a cosplay-esque warrior identity. But the songs remained good enough, and stylistically uniform enough, that they were more than able to stack a 75-minute set with fist-pumping anthems.

I came on board the Amon Amarth longship when a lot of other people did, with their fourth album, Versus The World. It opens with what’s still my favorite of their songs, “Death In Fire”:

That was the album they were supporting the first time I saw them. I’ve read in interviews that if it hadn’t succeeded, they were probably going to break up. Well, it turned out to be their breakthrough release, and as you can see from the description above, they’ve gotten bigger and bigger with each album. These days, they headline festivals in Europe, and play the largest clubs and theaters in the US. They’re not arena-sized over here yet, but I wouldn’t bet against them.

From the time of Versus The World on, Amon Amarth were honestly one of my favorite bands. I bought their albums With Oden On Our Side, Fate Of Norns, Surtur Rising, Deceiver Of The Gods, and Jomsviking as soon as they were announced. Amon Amarth was a brand I could trust. (Their 2008 album Twilight Of The Thunder God, one of their best releases, was released while I was the editor of Metal Edge magazine; several members of the group came by the office to promote it, so my copy is signed.)

They do basically one thing, and when they’re really dialed in, they’re the best on Earth at that one thing. All their songs are fist-pumping, hard-riffing metal anthems, set to a simple, pounding beat (they’re often described as death metal, but they don’t blast), with lyrics about Norse mythology and being a Viking warrior. Sometimes they get goofy — their 2022 album The Great Heathen Army has a song about a mythical goat — but other songs are surprisingly introspective, taking the listener deep inside the warrior’s mind and grappling with existential dread and despair.

And then they stumbled. Their 2019 album, Berserker, was the first one I didn’t buy a physical copy of. I downloaded the promo from the label, listened to it, and thought… Eh. It’s an Amon Amarth album. It’s fine. And when I saw them on tour that year, they played some songs from it, which fit into the set perfectly well, but I was really there for the old stuff. I still wanted to hear “Death In Fire.”

Their 2022 album, The Great Heathen Army, was an even bigger stumble. The first single was “Get In The Ring.” Watch the video.

What the fuck is that, I ask you? Quonset huts? Jeeps? Guns? What does any of this have to do with being a Viking? This (video and lyrics) is some Five Finger Death Punch-ass bullshit. And the rest of the album, while mostly sticking to their tried and true subject matter, just felt rote and unimaginative. I didn’t buy that one, either. Frankly, I was starting to lose interest in Amon Amarth entirely.

Well, my interest was rekindled about a year ago, when the song “We Rule the Waves” came out. Here’s the video:

Now that’s the old-school Amon Amarth I once loved. A big, dumb, chant-along song about being a Viking. No modern affectations in the video, pure cosplay. I’m sure it goes over huge at their shows.

Now, they’ve got a full album, The Allfather Awakens, coming out on Friday. The promo arrived in my inbox last week. They’ve made two other videos so far, for the opening track, “Gjällarhorn,” and “Eight Legs of Thunder.” Unfortunately, the latter is another attempt to modernize their image — with the horses, guns, and pickup trucks, it comes off like they’re hoping for a cameo on a Taylor Sheridan TV show. The song’s good, though.

Advertisement

And that’s the thing about the new album. They’ve written some genuinely good songs this time out, ones that sit among the best in their catalog, in fact. And they’ve experimented with some new ideas — “Upphaf” is an acoustic number, with female vocal harmonies, and it works really well. (It would have worked better if Hegg had sung it in a clean baritone; speak-singing the lyrics in his usual growl just makes him sound more like Cookie Monster than ever.) Some songs are slower, heavier, and doomier than usual; “Die With a War Cry” is pure punch-a-stranger-in-the-face. Others are faster and more aggressive than usual; “Oden’s Hunt” is practically thrash, absolutely charging along. And the last track, the nearly seven-minute “Ascending Like an Eagle,” is as epic and thrilling as its title. Track for track, this might be their best album since Twilight Of The Thunder God.

Earlier this year, Amon Amarth toured the US with what’s currently being passed off as Pantera. My brother went to see them in, I think, Maryland, and bought me a T-shirt. If The Allfather Awakens had sucked, I might have felt weird about wearing it. But I am fully back on board the Amon Amarth longship. And if you’ve never listened to them before, this album will make an excellent entry point.
